# The situation from Jamie's perspective

## Interpretive status

This is a public-safe reconstruction of Jamie's perspective derived from
private conversations and the proposal he prepared afterward. Jamie requested
the case study, but this draft still requires his line-by-line review. The
working source is not a certified or verbatim transcript. The account omits the
other participant's identity, employer, product domain, contact details,
precise chronology, staffing, commercial terms, family information, and
distinctive private anecdotes.

The goal is to make the situation legible to a reader with no prior context
without turning a private relationship into public evidence or making an
unresolved possibility sound like completed work.

## The situation

I was in a career transition, trying to understand how a long, unusual body of
participatory digital and documentation work could become legible as
professional value. I was often the person maintaining connective tissue:
records, decisions, follow-up, permissions, and the explanation that helped a
new participant enter the work.

That labor did not always resemble a familiar job title from the outside. From
inside the work, it was clearly product operations, facilitation, technical
implementation, knowledge stewardship, and organizational care. I designed
social and technical conditions that helped people act together. I wanted a
role where that capacity would be recognized, shared with colleagues, and
sustained without requiring me to hold the entire structure alone.

I was also exploring a broader research direction. Could the living knowledge
around a project become a source-backed wiki rather than a pile of disconnected
files? Could meetings, documents, people, claims, decisions, unresolved
questions, and evaluations remain linked? Could artificial intelligence help
with the labor of maintenance while people retained authority over meaning,
privacy, correction, and publication?

This was not abstract curiosity. I had developed versions of the practice in
real collective work where participants had limited time, different forms of
expertise, and different permissions. I knew what it meant for a valuable
question to disappear after a meeting, for a newcomer to lack history, or for a
public summary to require a safer source layer than the private record beneath
it.

## What happened

I had exploratory discussions with a technical and product leader whose
judgment I respected. He could join an expansive idea, then ask the practical
question that exposed whether it was yet a product: what does someone do with
it, why is that useful, and how is it materially different from a general tool?

Those questions did not feel like dismissal. They helped me see the distance
between my own rich use of a system and a clear offer another person could
adopt. I had evidence that the system mattered to me. I still needed evidence
that other people had the same problem, understood the value, and would choose
the practice over easier alternatives.

He also made me confront the political economy of information. A tool can
promise connection and collective intelligence while quietly collecting
people's private context for someone else's advantage. Lower friction can mean
less deliberate control. If my work claimed to be based in care, then consent,
access, attribution, retention, correction, and benefit could not be deferred
until later. They had to be part of the architecture.

I was looking for stable work and was honest about that. He gave me direct
product advice. Either find other people who already experience the problem I
experience, or conduct enough careful listening to discover which problem is
valuable to them. Build the offer around a precise need rather than asking
people to adopt the entire universe of my thinking.

The organization had a real knowledge problem. It needed to grow and execute,
yet important reasoning was not consistently written, organized, or available
for onboarding. The leader thought I might be able to help. There might be a
route to paid project work, but his authority and available resources were not
yet clear.

I felt the possibility strongly. It connected several parts of my life that had
often been read separately: technical building, documentation, group
facilitation, civic organizing, cultural work, archival practice, and careful
use of language models. Here was a leader inside a technical organization
naming a problem that I had been solving in less conventional environments.

That hope also carried emotional risk. I wanted the recognition to be real, and
I needed work. Those conditions could make a possibility feel more settled than
it was or make me over-produce in order to prove the whole future at once. I did
not want professional urgency to instrumentalize a valued relationship. The
proposal therefore had to create enough form for an honest decision while
leaving the other person free to say that the timing, authority, or fit was not
there.

I described my working practice: capture source material, track decisions and
open questions, scope access, create safer derivatives for different audiences,
and use documentation to support facilitation. I try to ensure that a person's
contribution does not disappear simply because they cannot attend every
conversation.

I was careful to distinguish this from speaking for the group. My role was to
support collective coherence, not to manufacture consensus. A raw transcript,
an internal working memory, an onboarding explanation, and a public statement
have different audiences and risks. The system should help people correct the
record and should retain uncertainty where no decision has been made.

Evaluation was part of the practice. A good prompt was not enough. The source
material, intended behavior, unfamiliar uses, attribution, decision status,
open questions, privacy, and human adoptability all needed inspection. A
polished summary could still fail the work.

The leader recognized a possible fit between this practice and the
organization's need. He also treated compensation as a prerequisite to
requesting substantial value. That preserved the dignity of the possible
collaboration.

I offered to prepare a proposal. The proposal translated the conversation into
a focused paid discovery and prototype. It named the organizational problem,
proposed starting from a very small approved source set, described concrete
artifacts, separated human review from automated drafting, and ended with a
decision to continue, revise, or stop.

My instinct was also to show the whole architecture: linked pages, sources,
decisions, permissions, evaluations, onboarding, and longer-term possibilities.
That instinct demonstrated depth, but it created a risk. The reader could have
to work too hard to find the first useful step. The later refinement of the
proposal has therefore been an exercise in product discipline: lead with the
pain, show one working loop, name the decision, and let the larger system become
visible through use.

## What mattered

The most important recognition was that my prior work was not merely
documentation around the edges of a project. It was operating infrastructure.
I had been designing how knowledge moved between people, how decisions remained
inspectable, how participation survived absence, and how private material
became appropriate public explanation. Those are real organizational
capabilities.

The conversation also gave me a demanding reader. He was receptive to the
ethical and cultural dimensions of the work, but he would not substitute shared
enthusiasm for a product argument. To earn a yes, I needed to show why this
practice was different from generic notes, how it could be adopted by a busy
team, and how a small test would produce evidence without requiring broad
access or a large commitment.

Finally, the possible project represented the kind of collaboration I wanted:
intellectually alive, technically serious, attentive to culture, and honest
about labor. I hoped it could become paid bridge work, a portfolio case, or even
a path toward a more lasting role. Those hopes were real, but they were not the
same as an outcome.

## Tensions and risks

- **Legibility versus fullness.** I wanted to communicate the entire practice,
  but the hiring or buying reader needed the simplest credible first step.
- **Care versus proof.** Ethical intent mattered, but I still needed evidence
  that the workflow improved real team behavior.
- **Preservation versus overload.** Keeping contributions available should not
  produce an undifferentiated archive that burdens the reader.
- **Relationship versus agreement.** Respect and creative affinity did not
  constitute authorization, budget, confidentiality, or a contract.
- **Portfolio value versus privacy.** I wanted the work to help explain my
  capabilities, while the underlying conversation and company context remained
  protected.
- **Automation versus representation.** Role-play and evaluation could reveal
  weaknesses in a draft, but only real people could approve how they were
  represented.

## What remained unresolved

The leader still had to determine whether he could authorize paid work. The
organization had not agreed to a project, selected an internal owner, approved
source material, or accepted terms. I still had to prove that the practice
could fit the team's existing habits and reduce, rather than add to, its work.

The truthful next move was a small, paid, permissioned test. Begin with one
approved source. Map where context was being lost. Produce a compact memory
structure with decisions, open questions, source references, and a correction
path. Ask whether it helped a newcomer understand the work and reduced repeated
explanation. Then continue, revise, or stop.

## What the evidence does not establish

The conversations and proposal do not establish that I was hired, that a paid
engagement occurred, that the organization adopted the method, that the leader
endorsed this account, or that any private company material entered the system.
They do not justify identifying the leader or organization.

What they do establish is narrower and still meaningful: a respected technical
leader independently named a team problem closely matched to my existing
practice, saw enough fit to explore a compensated engagement, and pressed me to
turn that practice into a focused, testable, adoptable offer.

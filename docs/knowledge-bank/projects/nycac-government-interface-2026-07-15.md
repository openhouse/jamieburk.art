# NYC Artist Coalition as a Government Interface

Reviewed: July 15, 2026

## Answer First

An official New York City Council transcript contains a direct reference from
DCLA Commissioner Tom Finkelpearl to NYC Artist Coalition. During the May 19,
2017 Fiscal 2018 Executive Budget hearing, he told the Council that the
coalition formed after DCLA hosted a January meeting for the DIY arts
community.

The surrounding testimony matters. Finkelpearl was explaining why CreateNYC's
public-engagement process was useful: it could deepen reciprocal relationships
with the public, expand direct feedback, and bring people together around a
common cause. The coalition was his concrete example of that method producing
an ongoing civic formation.

The record therefore supports a bounded institutional interpretation:

- **For DCLA**, the coalition made experience from informal cultural spaces
  more available to agency listening, planning, and implementation feedback.
- **For the Council**, the coalition helped turn distributed experience into
  organized testimony, data, problem definitions, and specific requests that
  could enter oversight and legislation.
- **For Council Member Rafael Espinal**, the coalition functioned as one
  collective constituency and implementation partner across public forums,
  cultural-space campaigns, hearings, and nightlife-policy work.

These are interpretations of the public record. They are not claims that any
official depended on Jamie, that the coalition represented every cultural
space, or that Jamie or the coalition alone caused legislation or agency
outcomes.

## The Recovered Council Reference

- **Hearing:** Fiscal 2018 Executive Budget, Committees on Finance, Cultural
  Affairs, and Libraries
- **Date:** May 19, 2017
- **Witness:** DCLA Commissioner Tom Finkelpearl
- **Locator:** transcript pages 91-92
- **Official record:** [New York City Council transcript](https://legistar.council.nyc.gov/View.ashx?GUID=68A2E207-53EA-43CA-A03E-F879A113464E&ID=5271559&M=F)

The direct proposition is narrow and strong: Finkelpearl identified NYC Artist
Coalition as having formed after DCLA convened the DIY arts community.

The surrounding propositions explain why he included the example:

1. DCLA wanted to supplement its relationships with roughly 1,000 funded
   nonprofit cultural organizations with more opportunities for direct public
   feedback.
2. The CreateNYC planning process was presented as an opportunity to rethink
   agency work through a closer reciprocal relationship with the public.
3. Finkelpearl used the coalition as an example of people organizing around a
   common cause after an agency convening.

This was budget testimony. The example made the value of public engagement
legible to Council members who were evaluating DCLA's work, priorities, and
resources. That last sentence is an institutional interpretation, not a quoted
statement of motive.

## Corroborating DCLA Record

Finkelpearl's official [Message from the Commissioner](https://www.nyc.gov/site/dcla/about/message-from-the-commissioner.page)
describes grassroots cultural groups and artists working in informal spaces,
then says CreateNYC office hours produced eye-opening interactions and helped
launch NYC Artist Coalition. He identifies its purpose as advocacy for DIY
spaces across the city and presents continuing direct dialogue as part of
keeping the cultural plan active.

Together, the Council testimony and DCLA message support an intermediary
reading: the coalition gave informal cultural-space participants an organized
form through which experience could travel into an agency process that
otherwise centered longstanding institutional and nonprofit relationships.

They do not establish that DCLA adopted every coalition recommendation or that
the coalition spoke for every informal space.

## Why DCLA Benefited

DCLA administers grants, capital projects, and cultural programs through formal
organizations. Much of the life under discussion here took place in informal,
small, volunteer-run, nightlife, or community-driven spaces whose problems cut
across buildings, fire safety, policing, licensing, affordability, and cultural
policy.

The coalition's institutional value was therefore not simply advocacy for an
already legible interest group. It helped:

- gather situated knowledge from people operating and using those spaces;
- translate distributed experiences into shared problems and proposals;
- create repeatable forums in which agency leaders could hear directly from
  affected people;
- keep a channel open after the original agency meeting;
- connect cultural concerns to agencies and policy domains outside DCLA's own
  authority.

This complements government capacity. It does not replace it.

## Why the Council Benefited

The Council's February 11, 2019 M.A.R.C.H. oversight hearing provides a second
official trace. The chair acknowledged written NYC Artist Coalition testimony;
Olympia Kazi described coalition work with agency-provided M.A.R.C.H. data and
said it had been shared with the Council; Jamie Burkart identified himself as a
coalition member and asked for talks rather than raids and greater transparency.

[Official M.A.R.C.H. hearing transcript](https://legistar.council.nyc.gov/View.ashx?GUID=2582E680-452D-46B1-8DE1-C5C5168F5D63&ID=7080592&M=F)

That record supports a deliberative-interface interpretation. The coalition
helped affected participants move from private or scattered experience to:

- testimony in a public record;
- a shared data and accountability question;
- concrete requests that an oversight committee could examine;
- follow-through across meetings, campaigns, and hearings.

The Council heard many witnesses. Coalition participation does not prove
Council agreement, exclusive representation, or sole causal effect.

## Why Espinal Benefited

Espinal's public record shows reciprocal engagement rather than one-way
outreach. In February 2019 he publicly thanked NYC Artist Coalition while
connecting its work to Save NYC Spaces and Talks Not Raids. Independent
reporting also places Council members, agency officials, cultural organizations,
and Jamie as a coalition speaker in the coalition-spearheaded Office of
Nightlife town hall.

The strongest bounded interpretation is that the coalition was one useful
implementation and constituency partner. It could convene affected people in
cultural spaces, keep public issue pathways active, translate experience into
specific asks, and return policy questions to a living community for discussion
and pressure.

The official records for the Office of Nightlife and Cabaret Law repeal
establish government outcomes and sponsors. They do not establish each
advocate's causal share. Espinal did not “need Jamie” in a provable
counterfactual sense; he benefited from a collective civic partner capable of
making dispersed cultural knowledge organized, public, and actionable.

## Jamie's Contribution

The wider knowledge bank separately supports Jamie's role in helping establish
and produce the coalition's recurring participation system: public event pages,
rotating cultural-space meetings, pre-meeting listening, practical support
sessions, hearings, and campaign action. That operating contribution helps
explain how a grassroots coalition could become usable to institutions without
claiming that Jamie authored every page, produced every event, or represented
every participant.

## Transcript Audit Boundary

The July 2026 audit searched indexed official Council attachments using Tom
Finkelpearl, the misspelling Finklepearl, NYC Artist Coalition, New York City
Artist Coalition, Artist Coalition, and DIY Arts Community. Five identified
official Finkelpearl transcript candidates were reviewed across CreateNYC,
budget, monuments, and Cultural Affairs hearings.

One direct coalition reference was recovered: May 19, 2017.

This is a bounded result, not a complete native Council transcript export.
Search indexing, OCR, spelling, attachment availability, and archival change
can hide records. “One recovered” must never be rewritten as “only one ever
existed.”

## Public Use

The direct Finkelpearl reference is defensible. The three institutional-value
claims are mature but held for deliberate composition. They should become
public-facing only when a specific portfolio argument benefits from them and
can preserve the distinctions among DCLA, the Council, Espinal, the coalition,
and Jamie.

The most compact safe synthesis is:

> NYC Artist Coalition helped make informal cultural-space experience usable
> in public institutions by translating it into recurring dialogue, testimony,
> data, and concrete policy requests. Official records show DCLA and Council
> members engaging with that collective work; they do not assign sole credit
> for government outcomes.

# NYC Artist Coalition Facebook Event Archival Production

**Preservation capture:** 2026-07-13
**Authenticated replay and source routing:** 2026-07-14

**Surface:** [NYC Artist Coalition Facebook events](https://www.facebook.com/nycartc/events)

**Control:** 34 past events displayed on event host cards
**Accounting:** 33 recovered event records + 1 unresolved control slot = 34/34 slots accounted

## Answer First

The surviving Facebook event record makes NYC Artist Coalition's operating
practice unusually concrete. The coalition did not only publish positions. It
created repeated occasions for people to gather, compare lived experience,
learn practical safety and legal pathways, form shared priorities, and carry
those priorities into public hearings, campaigns, and conversations with city
officials.

Jamie confirms that creating this public event and participation layer was a
substantial part of his contribution. The event pages document a collective
public system of invitations and routes; they do not by themselves establish
execution, attendance, or the individual author or producer of every page. The
public portfolio therefore uses **helped establish and produce**, preserves
collective credit, and keeps event-level authorship as an open research question.

For later reconstruction, each recovered event is retained as a public historical
plot point: who publicly hosted or associated with it, what it was called, where
or how it took place, when it occurred, what program it served, which public
paths it offered, and what remains unresolved. The bank keeps those event facts
separate from interpretation, individual authorship, attendance, and policy
causality.

## The Participation System

Twelve recovered records are recurring coalition meetings. Two were virtual;
the other ten name ten distinct physical venues:

- Magick City
- The Floasis
- Muchmore's
- The City Reliquary
- Shoestring Press
- Chinatown Soup
- Secret Project Robot
- Friends and Lovers
- Flowers for all Occasions
- Ode to Babel

That supports a **rotating-venue practice**, not an uninterrupted claim that a
meeting occurred every calendar month or that every coalition event used a new
venue.

The wider event population records a broader operating loop:

1. **Gather and listen.** General meetings invited participants to set
   priorities, propose working groups, and share what cultural spaces needed.
2. **Make support practical.** Events included Fire Guard test preparation,
   legal and architectural Q&A, community-space survey design, venue-support
   actions, and later pandemic relief coordination.
3. **Translate experience into an ask.** Meetings joined lived concerns with
   concrete campaigns around Cabaret Law repeal, CreateNYC, the Office of
   Nightlife, MARCH transparency, commercial lease protections, and Fair Rent
   NYC.
4. **Create a public path.** Event pages supplied a time, place, issue frame,
   action, and invitation to attend, testify, call, organize, or bring another
   concern into the coalition.
5. **Return and revise.** Later meetings reviewed policy developments, reported
   back, and set new priorities rather than treating a hearing or rally as the
   end of the work.

The February 2019 MARCH hearing event compressed the participation ethic into
four public words: **"Being there changes everything."** The same description
then asked participants to tell their stories. That is evidence of the event
layer's intended function; it is not evidence that Facebook responses became
testimony or that any one event caused a policy outcome.

## Interpretive Layer

Jamie describes this as a democratic listening and translation practice: believe
artists, gather in the places where culture is made, turn lived concerns into
practical support and shared priorities, and carry those priorities into civic
forums. He also describes events as an art form and cultural advocates as part
of the city's nervous system.

The event sequence is consistent with that interpretation. It does not make the
interpretation neutral platform metadata, establish every participant's
experience, or prove where each issue was first discovered. The Knowledge Bank
therefore retains this language as a **held interpretive claim**. It can guide
future research, collaborator notes, editorial composition, and photo retrieval
without becoming an unqualified public outcome claim.

## Public Engagement Signals

Thirty-two recovered event pages display a Facebook response total. The values
range from **9 to 1.7K**:

- 19 events display at least 100 responses;
- 9 display at least 400;
- 3 display at least 1K;
- 1 co-hosted resource webinar displays no response total in the recovered
  detail surface.

These figures are useful only as **event-level platform response signals**.
They are not unique-person or attendance counts.
They cannot be summed into people reached: responders may recur across events,
and a Facebook response does not establish arrival, duration, participation,
or attendance.

The October 2017 Market Hotel town hall demonstrates the boundary. Its event
page displays 1.2K responses. The event record and independently sourced
participation reporting remain distinct evidence layers. Do not convert Facebook
responses into turnout.

## Outbound Source And Action Network

Expanded descriptions in 25 recovered event records contain 61 outbound-link
occurrences. The [row-level link ledger](../data/nycartc-public-facebook-event-link-ledger.json)
normalizes these into 38 records:

- 7 published-article routes;
- 26 campaign, organization, action, registration, or public-resource leads;
- 1 protected working-document row with no locator exposed;
- 4 unresolved short-link rows.

The routes show that event pages joined a time and place to usable context: a
campaign site, testimony or hearing path, safety material, registration flow,
organization, or reported source. A posted URL does not prove that anyone read,
endorsed, registered through, or acted on it.

Five article routes were already present in the campaign press bank. This pass
also close-read and routed two additional articles:

- Melkorka Licea's April 8, 2017 New York Post report on Dance Liberation
  Network and NYC Artist Coalition Cabaret Law advocacy; and
- Elizabeth Kim's November 6, 2019 Gothamist report on the proposed Commercial
  Rent Stabilization bill, United for Small Business NYC input, and retail
  vacancy context.

## Claim Discipline

### Ready with boundaries

- 34 control slots are accounted for: 33 recovered and one unresolved.
- The recovered record spans January 2017 through January 2021.
- Twelve recurring meeting records span ten named physical venues and two
  virtual meetings.
- The event population documents a repeatable path from gathering and practical
  support to public action and institutional interface.
- The event descriptions created a reusable source and action network across 38
  normalized outbound-link records.
- Jamie describes his contribution as helping establish and produce that
  participation system; public records corroborate the system and specific
  organizing roles, not complete production responsibility.

### Held

- Do not claim Jamie individually authored or produced every event page.
- The coalition met every month without interruption.
- Every event took place at a different small cultural space.
- Do not claim Facebook response totals equal physical attendance or unique people reached.
- The event practice alone caused legislative or agency outcomes.
- The unresolved slot was deleted or had any particular date, title, or topic.
- Every participant understood the events as a democracy lab or listening
  practice.
- The event series by itself expanded public imagination or caused a policy
  result.

## Public-Safety Disposition

The research pass deliberately excluded:

- guest and attendee identities;
- invite and friend context;
- comments, reactions, and participant profile links;
- private group or account-administration context;
- contact details copied from descriptions;
- online meeting links, IDs, passcodes, and dial-in information;
- private analytics and credentials.

The public event ledger is intentionally row-level rather than aggregate-only:
it retains public dates, titles, venues or modes, formats, program
classifications, response displays, and event URLs so future sources and claims
can refer to stable historical plot points. That usefulness carries residual
reconstruction risk and is governed as a deliberate public-history choice. The
link ledger exposes public routes but removes tracking parameters and withholds
working-document, meeting-access, and unresolved goo.gl locators.

# NYC Artist Coalition, DCLA, And The City Council

## Recovered Council Reference

The strongest direct answer appears in the official transcript of the May 19,
2017, Fiscal 2018 Executive Budget hearing. DCLA Commissioner Tom Finkelpearl
described the agency's effort to build a closer reciprocal relationship with
the public, then cited NYC Artist Coalition's formation after a January DCLA
meeting for the DIY arts community as evidence of the value of direct public
feedback and bringing people together around a common cause.

This is institutional recognition, not a personal endorsement of Jamie. It
also does not mean DCLA created, owned, directed, or spoke for the coalition.

## What Jamie Did

Jamie's March 16, 2018, Council testimony makes the operating work unusually
clear. Testifying as an NYC Artist Coalition member and artist-safety advocate,
he supported increased DCLA funding to implement CreateNYC and described the
office-hours process as a two-way civic interface:

- artists, nonprofits, and public officials could form practical relationships;
- informal cultural communities could reach safety and compliance knowledge;
- the City could better understand how artists and informal spaces actually
  operated;
- coalition members could organize safety walkthroughs, Fire Guard support,
  recurring participation, recommendations, and public testimony.

That record supports a strong statement about Jamie's work: he helped make
informal cultural-space knowledge usable in public systems. He did so through
relational operations, safety practice, coalition infrastructure, public
communication, and testimony, not by claiming formal government authority.

## Why DCLA Benefited

DCLA's ordinary funding relationships centered heavily on nonprofit cultural
organizations and institutions. Informal, DIY, nightlife, and community-driven
spaces were often outside those relationships, dispersed across neighborhoods,
or wary of enforcement agencies. NYC Artist Coalition created a recurring,
organized interlocutor through which that field could define problems and
propose responses.

The public record supports three concrete benefits:

1. **Sensing:** DCLA gained access to lived operational knowledge from spaces
   that conventional grant and institutional channels did not reliably reach.
2. **Translation:** Coalition recommendations turned experiences of
   criminalization, administrative exclusion, safety risk, and displacement
   into a city-facing problem structure.
3. **Implementation feedback:** Recurring meetings and public testimony gave
   DCLA a way to hear whether cultural policy and public services worked in
   practice.

Finkelpearl's Council testimony and DCLA message both support this account. The
official CreateNYC appendix also preserves the coalition's recommendations on
criminalization, access to support, and affordability.

## Why The Council And Espinal Benefited

Councilmember Rafael Espinal's opening statement at the June 19, 2017, Cabaret
Law and Office of Nightlife hearing identifies the institutional problem. He
described DIY venues as culturally indispensable, named post-Ghost Ship
shutdown and enforcement concerns, and sought evidence from agencies,
advocates, venue operators, residents, and other affected people. He described
the proposed Office of Nightlife as a liaison among nightlife businesses,
government, and residents and as help navigating permits and legal
requirements.

The coalition and Jamie supplied one important strand of that plural record:
organized testimony, concrete safety experience, issue framing, affected-space
knowledge, and a constituency that could continue participating after one
hearing. This made a diffuse problem more governable without making the
coalition the only voice or giving it public authority.

The defensible answer is therefore institutional, not personal. The record does
not show that Finkelpearl, Espinal, DCLA, or the Council privately "needed
Jamie." It shows that government benefited from the civic interface Jamie
helped operate.

## Search Denominator

The July 16, 2026, research pass enumerated 30 official Council meeting records
indexed under Cultural Affairs from January 2017 through December 2019. Those
records exposed 50 transcript attachment entries representing 41 distinct
PDFs. Twenty-four distinct transcripts contained Finkelpearl's name.

Phrase-variant search and speaker-context review recovered one instance in
which Finkelpearl himself referred to NYC Artist Coalition: the May 19, 2017,
Executive Budget hearing. Two other Cultural Affairs transcripts contained
coalition references by other speakers. The known June 19, 2017, Consumer
Affairs hearing was reviewed separately; Finkelpearl was not the agency witness
there.

This is a bounded recovery result. It is not proof that no unindexed,
unavailable, image-only, or mistranscribed record exists elsewhere.

## Guardrails

- Do not say Finkelpearl endorsed Jamie personally.
- Do not say DCLA created or controlled NYC Artist Coalition.
- Do not say the coalition spoke for every artist or cultural space.
- Do not convert access, quotation, or an appendix into endorsement or adoption.
- Do not convert testimony, recommendations, or usefulness into policy causality.
- Preserve credit for coalition members, partner organizations, venue workers,
  public witnesses, agency staff, Council members, and prior advocates.

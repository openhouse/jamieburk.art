## Answer First

The surviving event record shows a coalition that did more than publish policy
positions. NYC Artist Coalition created repeated occasions for people to meet,
compare lived experience, learn practical safety and legal pathways, form shared
priorities, and carry those priorities into public hearings, campaigns, city
agencies, and conversations with elected officials.

Jamie identifies this recurring event and participation layer as a substantial
part of his contribution. The public record independently connects him to the
coalition, fire-code study groups, City Hall advocacy, and cultural-space work.
The event pages prove that the collective system existed and was used; they do
not identify the individual author or producer of every page or program. The
approved public wording is therefore **helped establish and produce**, with
collective and cohost credit preserved.

## Jamie's Role

Jamie describes the event practice as the place where lessons from WOW List's
"Being there changes everything" ethos met legislative advocacy. In his
first-hand account, rotating meetings among small cultural spaces created a way
to listen to artists, believe what they reported, make support practical, and
translate lived cultural experience into shared civic paths.

The defensible public claim is:

> Beginning in 2017, Jamie helped establish and produce NYC Artist Coalition's
> recurring participation system: public event pages, meetings rotating through
> small cultural spaces, practical safety and legal sessions, town halls,
> hearings, campaign actions, and relief convenings that connected artists'
> lived experience with civic pathways.

This claim rests on Jamie's first-hand account, the recovered event
population, independent reporting that identifies Jamie's fire-code study
groups and City Hall advocacy, and a contemporaneous interview connecting him
to coalition work. It does not assign Jamie sole authorship or production of
every event.

## Participation System

Twelve records are classified as recurring coalition meetings. Two were
virtual; the other ten name ten distinct physical cultural spaces:

- Magick City
- The Floasis
- Muchmore's
- The City Reliquary
- Shoestring Press
- Chinatown Soup
- Secret Project Robot
- Friends and Lovers
- Flowers for all Occasions
- Ode to Babel

The broader population contains:

- 29 events classified as participatory convenings;
- 15 with a government-interface signal;
- 15 at cultural or community spaces;
- nine at government venues;
- 24 cards displaying NYC Artist Coalition as organizer; and
- nine allied or cohosted listings.

The sequence makes an operating loop visible:

1. Gather and listen in cultural spaces.
2. Make support practical through safety preparation, legal and architectural
   questions, surveys, venue support, and resource coordination.
3. Translate lived concerns into concrete public asks and campaign language.
4. Create a route to a hearing, town hall, rally, agency meeting, or shared
   action.
5. Return, report back, revise priorities, and continue.

This supports a rotating-venue and recurring-practice claim. It does not prove
that a meeting occurred every calendar month, that every event used a different
venue, or that one event caused a policy outcome.

## Visible Traction

Thirty-two recovered event pages display a historical Facebook response count:

- 19 display at least 100 responses;
- seven display at least 500; and
- three display at least 1,000.

These are event-level interface labels. They are not verified attendance,
unique people, reach, participation, endorsement, conversion, mandate, or
impact. Responders may recur across events, and three displayed counts are
rounded in thousands. The counts are therefore not summed into a people-reached
claim.

When independent reporting supplies physical attendance for a particular event,
that source should govern the attendance claim. A Facebook RSVP is an interface
state, not a headcount inside a venue.

## Stakeholder And Mission Patterns

The event population documents repeated interfaces among artists, musicians,
venue operators, neighborhood spaces, cultural organizations, safety and legal
practitioners, small-business advocates, public agencies, Council members,
state legislators, and coalition partners.

The event topics include:

- venue safety and fire-guard capacity;
- CreateNYC and cultural planning;
- Cabaret Law repeal and Let NYC Dance;
- Office of Nightlife agenda-setting and accountability;
- M.A.R.C.H. transparency and Talks Not Raids;
- commercial-rent and displacement advocacy;
- venue-defense actions;
- small-business organizing; and
- COVID-era relief and mutual aid.

The event graph supports the existence of these interfaces. It does not turn a
host, speaker, public official, responder, or partner into an endorser of Jamie
or every coalition position.

## Public-Safety Boundary

The repository includes public event metadata and bounded aggregate findings.
It excludes:

- attendee and guest identities;
- invite and friend context;
- comments and reactions;
- historical emails, phone numbers, or personal contacts;
- meeting IDs, passcodes, and access links;
- private working-document links;
- account-administration and authenticated-session data; and
- raw event descriptions that contain protected details.

The public fixture records 13 withheld outbound-link occurrences by category so
their removal remains auditable without exposing them.

## Held Interpretive Layer

Jamie describes the practice as a kind of democracy lab: events as an art form,
small cultural spaces as places where the city can feel and name what hurts,
and advocacy as translation between cultural and civic codes. This framing is
preserved as Jamie's attributed interpretation. It is not presented as a
measured outcome, participant consensus, or external evaluation.

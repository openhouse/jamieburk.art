# Finkelpearl, NYC Artist Coalition, and Council Institutional Use

Date: 2026-07-15  
Status: public-safe research note; no website projection  
Scope: bounded official-record search and institutional-use interpretation

## Answer Up Front

A bounded search of official New York City Council records recovered one exact
instance in which Department of Cultural Affairs Commissioner Tom Finkelpearl
referred to NYC Artist Coalition in Council testimony.

On May 19, 2017, during an FY2018 Executive Budget hearing, Finkelpearl described
CreateNYC as an opportunity to build a reciprocal relationship with the public.
He said DCLA wanted to expand direct public feedback and used NYC Artist
Coalition's formation after DCLA's January DIY-arts meeting as an example of the
power of bringing people together around a common cause.

This is stronger than a generic acknowledgment. Finkelpearl placed the
coalition inside his account of what DCLA's public-engagement work produced and
why that work should continue. It does not establish that he or DCLA depended
on Jamie alone, that every coalition recommendation was adopted, or that the
coalition alone caused any later law, office, budget, or implementation choice.

## A Crucial Grammatical Boundary

Immediately after naming NYC Artist Coalition and a separate art-dealer
collaboration, Finkelpearl named the Disability Arts NYC taskforce. His next
sentence says that **it** had major influence on the plan and DCLA's disability
arts thinking. In context, that pronoun refers to the Disability Arts NYC
taskforce.

Do not attribute Finkelpearl's "huge influence" language to NYC Artist
Coalition. The coalition-specific evidence is already meaningful without
borrowing praise directed to another group.

## Why DCLA Needed The Work

### Direct record

Finkelpearl told the Council that DCLA wanted a closer reciprocal relationship
with the public and more direct feedback beyond its ongoing relationships with
funded cultural organizations. The City's final [CreateNYC
plan](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/dcla/downloads/pdf/cultureplan/createnyc-finalplan.pdf)
later said the DCLA DIY-spaces meeting spurred the coalition's establishment and
credited the coalition with recommendations and dialogue about preserving safe,
sustainable artist-led spaces.

### Evidence-based interpretation

DCLA needed a legible way to hear from informal, artist-led, and community
cultural spaces that ordinary grantmaking and institutional channels did not
fully represent. NYC Artist Coalition did more than submit isolated comments.
It helped turn a listening event into continuing civic capacity: follow-up
meetings, ranked priorities, recommendations, town-hall records, safety
resources, and a durable public surface.

That made the coalition useful to DCLA in three ways:

1. It extended a finite agency meeting into an ongoing relationship.
2. It translated dispersed experience into reviewable public recommendations.
3. It gave DCLA a concrete example that public engagement could generate new
   collective capacity rather than only a survey result.

## Why The Council Needed The Work

Council needs more than broad sentiment. It needs affected-community testimony,
specific mechanisms, records that can be entered into a hearing, and questions
that can be put to agencies.

Two official transcripts show the coalition supplying those forms.

### Cabaret Law hearing

At the [June 19, 2017 Committee on Consumer Affairs
hearing](https://legistar.council.nyc.gov/View.ashx?GUID=41F1062B-FC32-4A12-846E-65CEB3BB052C&ID=5316935&M=F),
chaired by Rafael Espinal, Jamie testified as a NYC Artist Coalition member. He
connected fire-safety work and direct experience with cultural spaces to a
specific institutional mechanism: otherwise code-compliant spaces could be
afraid to approach FDNY because Cabaret Law exposure undermined trust. He asked
for full repeal and for low-income community spaces to have seats in nightlife
governance. Espinal thanked the panel and Jamie for sharing the personal story.

This made an abstract enforcement question concrete. Jamie brought affected
experience, a safety practice, a diagnosis of institutional mistrust, a
legislative request, and a governance-design request into one public record.

### MARCH oversight hearing

At the [February 11, 2019 Committee on the Justice System
hearing](https://legistar.council.nyc.gov/View.ashx?GUID=2582E680-452D-46B1-8DE1-C5C5168F5D63&ID=7080592&M=F),
Chair Rory Lancman used NYC Artist Coalition's FOIL-based MARCH analysis to
question NYPD. NYPD disputed the coalition's first finding and raised concerns
about how operations were classified.

That disagreement matters. It shows that coalition research was institutionally
usable and publicly contestable. Council could use it to expose a data and
definition problem, ask an agency to answer, and create a record for later
legislative and oversight work. Institutional use is not the same as blanket
endorsement.

## Why Espinal Needed The Coalition And Jamie

This is an interpretation of the public record, not a statement about Espinal's
private motives.

Espinal was trying to legislate across a gap between formal nightlife
regulation and the lived operation of small cultural spaces. The coalition
could help him:

- identify harms and safety contradictions that agency testimony might not
  surface;
- bring people affected by the law into the formal hearing record;
- connect repeal to concrete safety and trust mechanisms;
- widen the imagined constituency beyond licensed commercial venues; and
- propose representation for low-income community spaces in future nightlife
  governance.

Jamie was useful in that setting because he could translate between cultural,
technical, operational, and civic codes. He did not offer only opposition. He
described what the coalition had tried, what direct work with spaces revealed,
what institutional relationship was failing, what law should change, and who
should be included next.

## Strongest Defensible Professional Interpretation

Jamie's work mattered to DCLA and the Council because he helped turn
cultural-space experience into public-safety practice, formal testimony,
concrete governance requests, and durable public records that officials could
reference. The record shows institutional usefulness, not dependence on Jamie
alone or sole policy causality.

This is a Product Operations and Implementation story as much as an advocacy
story:

- listen across differently situated participants;
- identify the underlying operating failure;
- organize repeatable safety and participation practices;
- translate lived experience into legible requirements and decisions;
- create public artifacts that can survive a meeting;
- preserve collective authorship and methodological disagreement; and
- carry the work into the next institutional handoff.

## Publication Boundaries

- Do not say Finkelpearl called NYC Artist Coalition a huge influence. That
  phrase refers to the Disability Arts NYC taskforce.
- Do not say Finkelpearl, DCLA, Espinal, or the Council personally needed Jamie
  or depended on him alone.
- Do not assign Jamie authorship of the coalition's MARCH analysis without
  role-specific evidence.
- Do not repeat the transcript's 100-percent Fire Guard exam-pass statement as
  an independently verified metric until its records and wording are
  reconciled.
- Do not convert testimony, acknowledgment, research use, or later enactment
  into sole causality.
- Retain collaborator and institutional credit, including Olympia Kazi, Tara
  Mc Manus, other coalition members, DCLA, Council chairs, and agency witnesses.
- Keep this material in the knowledge bank until a separate governed projection
  decision is made. It creates no `/proofs` or archive route.

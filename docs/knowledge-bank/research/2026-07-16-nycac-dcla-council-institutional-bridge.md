# NYC Artist Coalition as a DCLA and Council institutional bridge

Date: 2026-07-16

## Question

Where did Tom Finkelpearl refer to NYC Artist Coalition in New York City
Council testimony, and what do official records support about why the
coalition's work mattered to DCLA, the Council, and Council Member Rafael
Espinal?

## Finding

The decisive exact named reference is in Finkelpearl's sworn testimony at the
[May 19, 2017 Fiscal 2018 Executive Budget hearing](https://legistar.council.nyc.gov/View.ashx?GUID=68A2E207-53EA-43CA-A03E-F879A113464E&ID=5271559&M=F).
While explaining how CreateNYC could support a more reciprocal relationship
with the public, he said NYC Artist Coalition formed after DCLA's January DIY
arts meeting. He presented the coalition alongside other newly activated groups
as evidence that public engagement could create continuing civic capacity, not
only collect comments.

Two other Finkelpearl appearances describe the same operating relationship
without using the coalition's formal name in the relevant passages:

- At the [February 27 Cultural Plan - Next Steps hearing](https://legistar.council.nyc.gov/View.ashx?M=F&ID=5010771&GUID=AF871425-F776-4CD2-89EC-201202A95D97),
  he said members of the DIY community submitted formal recommendations,
  continued organizing independently, and created a relationship DCLA wanted
  to sustain.
- At the [September 20 Comprehensive Cultural Plan hearing](https://legistar.council.nyc.gov/View.ashx?M=F&ID=5479046&GUID=E9387EDE-DCBA-455B-B3A0-71F30D23DF3E),
  he described DIY arts spaces as a group activated around the plan and said
  the process gave DCLA an opportunity to listen. Coalition testimony later in
  the same hearing supplied the formal name and said recommendations were
  included in the plan.

DCLA's official [CreateNYC NYC Artists page](https://createnyc.cityofnewyork.us/the-cultural-plan/issue-areas/nyc-artsts/)
makes the institutional identification explicit: it says the January meeting
spurred NYC Artist Coalition's establishment and credits the coalition with
organizing recommendations and driving dialogue about safe, sustainable
artist-led spaces.

## Why DCLA benefited

This section is an evidence-based institutional interpretation, not a claim to
know Finkelpearl's private motives.

DCLA already had durable relationships with funded nonprofit cultural
organizations. Informal and DIY spaces did not necessarily appear through the
same grantee, compliance, or executive channels. NYC Artist Coalition helped
turn dispersed experience into a legible and continuing interface:

- people could gather and name problems in their own cultural settings;
- recommendations could be drafted and delivered in a form an agency could
  answer;
- independent organizing could continue between agency meetings;
- DCLA could test whether its public process reached beyond established
  institutions;
- the resulting feedback could enter a citywide plan without requiring the
  coalition to become an agency program.

That is why Finkelpearl's May reference was useful in a budget hearing. The
coalition was evidence that the planning process produced reciprocal public
engagement and newly organized civic capacity. It helped him answer the
Council's practical question: was this engagement work worth the time and
public investment?

## Why the Council benefited

The Council had legislative, budget, and oversight authority. It still needed
constituents who could make emerging problems visible, translate experience
into proposals, and enter those proposals into a public record.

NYC Artist Coalition provided a repeatable pathway from cultural-space
experience to formal civic deliberation. The
[September 14 Intro 1652 hearing transcript](https://legistar.council.nyc.gov/View.ashx?M=F&ID=5444684&GUID=6DBE5276-1842-4693-843F-18667D2D6EBC)
records Olympia Kazi, Jamie Burkart, and another coalition participant as
witnesses. The coalition's safety, equity, and repeal arguments therefore
became material Council Members and staff could question, cite, compare, and
act on.

The Council did not need agreement from every participant. It needed an
organized, public, contestable record. The coalition helped supply one.

## Why Espinal benefited

Espinal answered this directly. In the
[June 21 stated meeting](https://legistar.council.nyc.gov/View.ashx?GUID=85421758-71D8-4ED5-9D27-C7B5126089FD&ID=5330214&M=F),
he credited Dance Liberation Network and NYC Artist Coalition for their
on-the-ground work bringing attention to Intro 1652, then urged colleagues to
sign on.

The institutional value is concrete:

- Espinal supplied legislative authorship and a route through Council;
- advocates supplied lived evidence, public attention, testimony, safety and
  equity framing, and visible constituency support;
- the coalition gave a public identity and repeatable coordination structure
  to work that no one office could manufacture on its own.

The [official legislative record](https://legistar.council.nyc.gov/LegislationDetail.aspx?ID=3086319&GUID=6FDA3305-06B3-47B3-9DF6-21B605C5A8EE)
identifies Espinal as prime sponsor, lists 25 Council sponsors, and records
enactment as Local Law 214 of 2017. Those facts establish sequence and outcome,
not a causal percentage for Jamie or the coalition.

## Jamie's contribution

The public-safe professional claim is not that government needed Jamie as an
irreplaceable individual. It is that Jamie helped build and steward the
operating layer government actors demonstrably found useful:

- public invitation and accessible issue framing;
- listening in small cultural spaces;
- collaborative drafting and collective naming;
- recurring meetings and follow-up;
- public campaign identities and action paths;
- source-backed testimony and policy communications;
- continuity between agency dialogue, Council hearings, and public action.

This is technical project management and product operations in a civic-cultural
setting: emerging concerns acquired durable forms that collaborators, agency
staff, legislators, and the public could use.

## Boundaries

- Do not say DCLA created or controlled NYC Artist Coalition.
- Do not say Finkelpearl used the formal coalition name in every relevant
  hearing. The exact named instance recovered here is May 19; the February and
  September passages are functional descriptions.
- Do not say the Council needed Jamie personally or that Espinal could not have
  advanced Intro 1652 without him.
- Do not say Jamie authored every recommendation, event, campaign surface, or
  testimony.
- Do not say Jamie or NYC Artist Coalition alone caused sponsorship, passage,
  or repeal.
- Preserve Dance Liberation Network, coalition collaborators, witnesses,
  cultural-space hosts, DCLA staff, Council Members, movement partners, and the
  wider public in the account.

## Audit scope

The pass used exact-name transcript search and close reading of the directly
implicated 2017 Council records: February 27, May 19, June 21, September 14,
and September 20, plus DCLA's official CreateNYC page and the Intro 1652
legislative record. This supports a recovered exact instance and a bounded
institutional interpretation. It is not a claim that every Council document in
existence was machine-audited.

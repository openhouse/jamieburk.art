# NYC Artist Coalition as a Government Interface

Date: July 15, 2026

## Question

Did DCLA Commissioner Tom Finkelpearl refer to NYC Artist Coalition in City
Council testimony? What do public records establish about why the coalition's
work mattered to DCLA, the Council, and Council Member Rafael Espinal?

## Direct Finding

Yes. One direct Finkelpearl reference was recovered in the official May 19,
2017 joint Finance and Cultural Affairs budget-hearing transcript.

On transcript pages 91-92, Finkelpearl described the CreateNYC planning process
as an opportunity to reimagine DCLA's work through a closer reciprocal
relationship with the public. He distinguished continued work with roughly
1,000 funded nonprofit cultural organizations from a desire to expand direct
public feedback. He then cited NYC Artist Coalition's formation after a
DCLA-hosted January DIY arts-community meeting while describing the power of
bringing people together around a common cause.

This is direct official recognition in sworn Council testimony. It does not
require us to infer that the coalition was visible or useful to DCLA; the
commissioner chose it as an example in his formal account of the agency's public
engagement.

## Council Use

The February 11, 2019 Committee on the Justice System transcript shows a
different and more operational form of value. Council Chair Rory Lancman:

- identified NYC Artist Coalition's Freedom of Information Law request and its
  mapped 2012-2017 MARCH data;
- used the coalition's finding that 48 percent of raids produced no enforcement
  action to question NYPD;
- emphasized that the analysis came from FOIL documents rather than anecdote;
- used the data to question raid selection, the value of operations, and racial
  disparities; and
- proposed a meeting among NYPD, the coalition, interested Council members, and
  Council staff to compare evidence directly.

The coalition's work therefore entered the hearing as oversight infrastructure.
It supplied an independent empirical record, sharpened questions to an agency,
and created a pathway for follow-up after the hearing.

## Why the Work Mattered

### To DCLA

DCLA already had relationships with funded institutions. NYC Artist Coalition
made an emergent constituency of DIY venues, community-driven spaces, artists,
and cultural workers more coherent and reachable without pretending that those
participants were one homogeneous community. The coalition could receive an
invitation, convene people, turn concerns into recommendations, publish them,
and return to the agency with continuity.

Finkelpearl's own framing supports this interpretation: he placed the coalition
inside DCLA's effort to expand direct feedback and build reciprocal public
relationships. The City-hosted CreateNYC appendix separately shows coalition
recommendations entering the plan's public record.

### To the City Council

The Council needed more than agreement. It needed public testimony, organized
constituency knowledge, independent evidence, and follow-up partners. The 2017
Cabaret hearing supplied testimony about trust and safety from within cultural
spaces. The 2019 MARCH hearing shows the Council using coalition research to
test agency accounts and design a next step.

The defensible formulation is that the Council used and benefited from NYC
Artist Coalition's testimony, research, and sustained civic presence. The
record does not support saying the Council depended solely on the coalition.

### To Rafael Espinal

Espinal sponsored the Office of Nightlife and Cabaret Law repeal measures in a
field where implementation depended on the lived realities of venues, artists,
workers, residents, agencies, and enforcement systems. NYC Artist Coalition
offered a durable way for cultural-space participants to gather, make
recommendations, testify, and evaluate what new governance should protect.

This is a bounded institutional interpretation. The reviewed sources document
the coalition's public convenings, Jamie's testimony, Espinal's legislative
role, and the Office of Nightlife's liaison purpose. They do not quote Espinal
saying that he needed Jamie or the coalition, and they do not allocate sole
causal credit.

## Jamie's Contribution

Jamie helped make the interface durable. Separately supported knowledge-bank
claims establish his role in co-founding NYC Artist Coalition; creating its
logo and shared public identity; building the coalition and campaign website
family; helping establish recurring participation routes; testifying publicly;
and building documentation and policy-communications infrastructure.

That operating work matters because public knowledge must persist long enough
to become usable. A recognizable identity, maintained websites, repeatable
meetings, public calls to action, source trails, and handoffs allowed coalition
knowledge to move across people and settings rather than disappearing after one
conversation.

## Source Boundary

This was a bounded official-record search, not a guaranteed census of every
Council attachment or imperfectly indexed historical scan. The pass searched
the Council's indexed records and closely read three full transcripts:

- May 19, 2017 joint Finance and Cultural Affairs budget hearing: one direct
  Finkelpearl reference recovered;
- June 19, 2017 Cabaret Law hearing: no Finkelpearl reference recovered; DCLA
  was represented by Deputy Commissioner Kristen Sakoda; and
- February 11, 2019 MARCH hearing: no Finkelpearl reference recovered, but
  repeated Council use of coalition research was recovered.

`Not recovered` is not the same as `did not exist`. Future official records or
collaborator testimony may deepen the account.

## Public Wording

> NYC Artist Coalition became a durable interface between cultural spaces and
> city government. In 2017, DCLA Commissioner Tom Finkelpearl cited its
> formation in Council testimony while explaining the value of expanded public
> feedback. In 2019, Council Chair Rory Lancman used the coalition's FOIL-derived
> MARCH analysis to question NYPD and proposed a follow-up meeting with the
> coalition.

## Held Wording

Do not publish claims that Finkelpearl, DCLA, the Council, or Espinal personally
needed Jamie or depended solely on NYC Artist Coalition. Those formulations
collapse observed institutional use into unsupported motive and dependency.

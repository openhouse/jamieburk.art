# Jamie's Perspective

## The work I was trying to name

I came to these conversations during a career transition. I had spent years
building software, photographing communities, organizing coalitions,
facilitating meetings, maintaining archives, and creating the practical systems
that let shared work continue. These activities were not separate careers to
me. They were different ways of asking how people perceive one another, how a
group remembers, and how technology can help a community act without erasing
the sources of its knowledge.

I could feel the coherence from inside the practice. It was harder to make that
coherence immediately legible to another person, especially someone applying a
product test. I was inclined to begin with the deepest account: language,
culture, multiple voices, the body, recognition, and the history embedded in a
technical form. My friend kept asking what a person would do with the thing and
why it would matter to them.

Those questions were not a dismissal of the theory. They were a form of care.
They required me to find the smallest useful encounter through which the larger
idea could become visible.

## From a private knowledge environment to a team practice

I had been developing an editable knowledge environment for my own work. It
combined source material, notes, claims, citations, corrections, and distinct
analytical perspectives. Instead of treating a model's context as an invisible
pile of retrieved text, I wanted people to be able to see and shape the pages
through which a system understood a project. The knowledge base remained
editable. Sources retained identity and access conditions. Claims could be
corrected. Different readers could make different interpretations without
silently becoming one authoritative voice.

I understood this as part of a longer cultural tradition of shared knowledge,
not simply as an interface around a model. My friend could see that, along with
the gap between a personally compelling research system and a product another
team would choose to use.

My friend asked how the perspectives were created, how this differed from
uploading a folder to a generic assistant, and which problem justified the
additional care. The observation was that I was clearly one user, perhaps the
best-understood user, but one person is not a market. Two paths followed: find
other people who
already shared the problem, or conduct a listening tour without insisting that
the existing solution be the answer.

I took that seriously. My research needed contact with situations in which the
cost of lost context was already visible.

## The real situation arrived

Later, my friend entered a leadership role at a small, rapidly growing
creative-technology company. My friend believed in the company's attempt to join
technical invention with a more responsible relationship to culture and
creative participation. But the organization was struggling to maintain the
writing and knowledge practices required to clarify its product and bring new
engineers into the work.

My friend described the problem plainly: important ideas and decisions were not being
documented well enough. The team needed to know what it was making and why. It
needed to execute. Growth would intensify the cost of ambiguity. More artificial
intelligence would not by itself fix weak habits of collective thought.

I felt the research click into place. This was not a request for an all-purpose
knowledge platform. It was a living example of a problem I had encountered in
many forms: people are doing sophisticated work, but the pathways among source,
conversation, decision, responsibility, and next action are fragile. When those
pathways break, the organization loses both intelligence and trust.

## What my civic work had taught me

In coalition work, I often became a kind of movement librarian and practical
help desk. Meetings brought together people with different histories,
institutional roles, vocabularies, and stakes. A useful record had to do more
than summarize. It had to help someone understand how a decision emerged, what
was agreed, who was responsible, what remained contested, and which material
could or could not travel.

The infrastructure included ordinary things: agendas, shared drives, scoped
permissions, transcripts, minutes, contact context, research notes, action
lists, websites, and email systems. What made them powerful was their
configuration. A shared digital surface could help a coalition speak clearly,
welcome a newcomer, route a question, and preserve the work contributed by
people who were not always visible in the final public moment.

I cared especially about contributions that disappear. Someone offers an idea
in a meeting. The room recognizes it. Then the work moves on and no one can see
where that idea went. Repeated often enough, that experience teaches people
that participation is ceremonial rather than consequential.

A source-backed team memory can answer a different promise: your contribution
will not automatically control the outcome, but it will be held in relation to
the work. Another person can find it, understand its context, see whether it
became a decision or an open question, and continue from there.

That is not just documentation. It is operating structure for collective
agency.

## Why the company's cultural stakes mattered

The company's product depended on culture, creative work, and communities. My
friend was thinking seriously about attribution, participation, economics, and
the danger of treating archives as an extractive resource. Those were not
adjacent concerns for me. They matched the source-custody questions already
inside my practice.

A source is not merely content available to a system. It has an origin, a
holder, an access condition, and a social life. Permission to inspect something
is not permission to publish it. Evidence for a claim is not permission to use
that evidence for every audience. A correction should travel to the outputs it
affects. A protected absence can be part of an honest record.

The proposed work therefore had to join usefulness with restraint. It could
not succeed by capturing the most material. It had to select carefully, preserve
provenance, and return forms that the team could actually inspect and correct.

## What I believed I could offer

I could begin with listening rather than a predetermined installation. I could
help the team identify one recurring point where knowledge was being lost: a
product decision, an onboarding question, a meeting series, or a repeated
disagreement whose history no one could easily reconstruct.

With explicit source approval, I could map the small set of materials and human
roles needed to understand that friction. I could then produce a compact
operating-memory packet, such as:

- a start-here page explaining the question and current state;
- a decision record showing rationale, source support, dissent, and remaining
  uncertainty;
- an open-question list with responsibility and next action;
- source links, access notes, and correction paths;
- a handoff test for a teammate who was not in the original conversation;
- a recommendation to continue, revise, or stop.

The test mattered. The measure was not whether the packet sounded intelligent.
Could a new teammate find an answer? Could they tell how the team knew it? Could
they identify what remained open or protected? Could they submit a correction?
Could they continue the work without needing a private reconstruction from the
person who had been in every meeting?

## What the opportunity meant to me

The conversation was professionally significant because it made a path visible
between my accumulated practice and a kind of work another organization might
value. I was seeking sustainable, compensated work after years of carrying a
large share of the operational responsibility for mission-driven projects. I
did not want to abandon the care, creativity, or public purpose of that history.
I wanted to bring them into a healthier form of collaboration.

My friend was careful not to turn my eagerness into free labor, saying in
substance that payment authority had to be established before asking me to do
more. That was both a practical condition and an expression of respect.

I was also candid that I wanted the work to become legible in my professional
portfolio. A well-scoped engagement could serve the company and help me
demonstrate a real capability: translating complex, culturally situated work
into operating structures that another team can use.

The relationship was not purely transactional. There was friendship,
intellectual kinship, humor, and genuine pleasure in thinking together. That
made the commercial line more important, not less. Clear scope and compensation
would protect the relationship as well as the work.

## The risks I needed to avoid

I did not want to treat a rich conversation as proof that a product had been
validated. I did not want to imply that the company had hired me, approved a
budget, adopted the method, or received a completed pilot. I did not want to
publish private company context or use my friend's identity as borrowed
credibility.

I also did not want the sophistication of the architecture to obscure the
first useful step. The three-graph model—meaning, evidence, and source
custody—helps me reason about the system. A sponsor first needs to see a small
engagement that improves one real handoff.

Finally, I had to honor the limits of the source itself. A corrected working
transcript is not a certified record of every word. Recording stopped when the
conversation entered material that should not be retained. The case study must
not reconstruct what was intentionally left outside it.

## Where the story honestly ends

I left the conversations with a stronger proposal, a clearer prospective user,
and a real organizational problem against which the practice could be tested.
My friend left with a possible way to address a difficult team need and a
commitment to determine whether compensated work could be authorized.

No recovered source establishes that the authorization, contract, or work
followed. The accomplishment at this stage is not a completed engagement. It is
the emergence of a truthful, focused hiring argument from years of situated
practice: I can help a team turn scattered thought into inspectable,
correctable, usable operating memory—without separating knowledge from the
people, rights, and histories that give it meaning.

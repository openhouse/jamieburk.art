# The Prospective Sponsor's Perspective

## Who I am in this story

I am a product and engineering leader who has spent a long time around digital
products, creative work, and the institutions that gather attention and power.
I know how quickly an organization can become very good at producing activity
without becoming equally good at knowing what it is doing, why it is doing it,
or what it has learned.

I was also in a period of professional change. I wanted to move away from work
whose scale, incentives, or pace had become difficult to reconcile with a good
life. I was attracted to earlier-stage work: listening carefully, discovering
what a product should be, and helping a small group make consequential choices.
I wanted work that was technically ambitious and culturally responsible, with
real consideration for the people whose creative lives made the product
possible.

That context matters because I did not encounter Jamie as a conventional
consultant presenting a finished service. I encountered a friend and peer whose
practice was unusually difficult to fit into a familiar box. Jamie moved among
software, photography, civic organizing, archives, facilitation, and systems of
care. The questions were compelling, but the immediate product proposition was
not always easy to see.

## The early conversations

My first instinct was to ask basic product questions. What is the thing? What
does a person do with it? Why would they use it? Who has the problem strongly
enough to change a habit or pay for help?

Jamie often answered from the inside of the work. The answers connected
software to language, memory, nervous systems, cultural inheritance, and the
many voices that coexist within a person or community. I found that intellectual
range meaningful, but I also kept returning to use. A product cannot live on a
cosmology alone. It has to help a particular person accomplish something they
could not accomplish, or could not accomplish well, before.

One of Jamie's concrete tools made this tension productive. A system for
reviewing and selecting photographs had an evident user activity, a visible
output, and many possible variations. My advice was to let different people try
the variations and observe what resonated. The path forward was not to explain
the entire theory first. It was to create an experience, watch how people used
it, and learn.

The same test applied to Jamie's team-memory research. Jamie described a
personal or team knowledge environment built from editable pages, source
material, citations, and a cast of analytical perspectives. A person could
work with the system in ordinary language while retaining more control over
context, correction, and custody than a generic chat window usually provides.

I could restate the idea back to Jamie: a familiar shared-knowledge tradition,
combined with computational readers and editors, cultivated around a particular
body of work. I could also see the unresolved questions. How are those
perspectives constructed? What is meaningfully different from giving a large
model a folder of documents? Which controls will people actually use? What
problem is urgent enough to make the additional structure worthwhile?

My concern was not that the research lacked imagination. It was that Jamie was
both the builder and the clearest current user. That is legitimate evidence of
a problem, but it is not yet broad demand. Jamie could either find more people
who already felt the same pain or temporarily loosen attachment to the solution
and conduct a listening tour. The goal of that tour would be to discover a
problem important enough that a focused solution could become unavoidable.

## The company situation that made the research practical

Later, I joined a small, rapidly growing creative-technology company. I had
chosen it partly because its ambitions joined technical invention with cultural
responsibility. The work raised difficult questions about attribution,
participation, value, and the relationship between a product and the people
whose culture and creative labor animate it. I believed the company was trying
to design those relationships more responsibly than many larger platforms.

At the same time, the organization had a serious operating problem. Ideas,
decisions, rationale, and foundational knowledge were not being captured in a
way that supported execution. The product still needed greater definition. The
team was growing. New engineers would need to understand not only what to build,
but why. Existing writing and documentation habits were too weak for the amount
of complexity the team was taking on.

The problem was not primarily a lack of artificial intelligence. Adding another
model to the workflow would not create the missing habit of writing, preserve
the history of a decision, or make disagreement legible. The organization
needed practices that helped people think together and left useful traces for
the next person.

This is when Jamie's unusual combination of experiences became newly relevant.
The same work that could seem diffuse in the abstract looked coherent against a
real organizational need.

## Why Jamie seemed suited to the problem

Jamie had spent years creating shared infrastructure for groups whose work did
not begin as tidy institutional knowledge. In civic coalitions, a meeting was
not merely a calendar event followed by generic minutes. It was a site where
many constituencies brought distinct histories, needs, and forms of authority.
The record had to support action while respecting permissions, credit, and
disagreement.

Jamie described a practice that could include scoped source access, agendas,
transcripts, derivative minutes, decision records, research notes, and
person-specific context. The aim was not to ingest everything indiscriminately.
It was to help a group recover what had happened, understand what remained open,
and direct a contribution toward action without losing the contributor.
Jamie sometimes named this role a movement librarian: the person who helps a
group find what it knows, understand how it knows it, and put that knowledge to
work.

That last quality mattered to me. In many organizations, a useful idea is
acknowledged and then disappears because no one knows where it belongs. People
lose trust when their contributions repeatedly vanish. A more comprehensive,
self-service memory could let someone ask a question, trace how the work reached
its present state, and see how their contribution might move it forward.

Jamie also treated archives and culture as living responsibilities rather than
raw material waiting to be extracted. That aligned with the company's ethical
stakes. Source custody, attribution, permission, and correction were not
compliance decorations added after product discovery; they shaped what could be
made and how.

## The possible engagement

I was interested enough to describe the organizational problem directly and to
ask Jamie to show how the practice might apply. A sensible first engagement
would not be a company-wide knowledge platform. It would be a focused discovery
or prototype around one recurring knowledge friction.

Such an engagement might:

- identify where product rationale, decisions, and open questions are being
  lost;
- map a small, explicitly approved source set and the team's actual working
  habits;
- return a compact start-here view, a decision record, open questions,
  provenance, correction paths, and access notes;
- test whether a teammate who was not in the room can find an answer, trace it,
  understand uncertainty, and propose a correction;
- end with a clear choice to continue, revise, or stop.

The value would not be measured by the volume of material captured. It would be
measured by whether the team could think and execute with less context loss,
whether onboarding became more truthful and useful, and whether the practice
fit existing work rather than creating a burdensome parallel bureaucracy.

## The commercial and relational line

I did not want friendship or intellectual generosity to become a route to
unpaid consulting. Jamie had already given substantial thought to the problem.
If the company wanted more, I needed to determine whether an authorized budget
and a real sponsor existed before asking Jamie to invest further labor.

That statement was meaningful, but it was not an offer. I still needed internal
authorization. Scope, confidentiality, intellectual property, source access,
compensation, timing, and success conditions all required human agreement. The
conversations did not establish that those approvals occurred.

I also respected the boundary of the conversation itself. When the discussion
moved toward work that should not be recorded, I asked for recording to stop.
That was part of the same ethic: a useful knowledge system does not assume that
everything accessible should be retained or reused.

## Where the story honestly ends

The story ends with a credible problem, mutual interest, a plausible focused
pilot, and unresolved commercial authority. It does not end with a contract,
completed work, organizational adoption, or endorsement.

What feels most accurate is the double recognition. I recognized that Jamie's
apparently varied practice could answer a pressing organizational need. Jamie
recognized that the organization's difficulty was the kind of real problem the
research had been seeking. The next truthful step was not to inflate that
recognition into a success story. It was to turn it into a clear, compensable,
testable proposal and wait for an authorized decision.

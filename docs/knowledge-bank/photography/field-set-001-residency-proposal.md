# Release Yourself Into the Archive

> **Authorship status:** This is an AI-assisted first-person draft composed from
> Jamie's July 22, 2026 reflection and the governed photography notebook. It
> awaits Jamie's line-by-line review before it may be treated as his final
> artist statement. Its Wiki publication records working intent; it does not
> approve photographs or authorize a portfolio projection.

## Working proposal

I propose to spend up to two weeks in residence at 196 with a private,
rough-draft field of approximately 1,000 photographs from my lifetime archive.
The residency takes place in the apartment in Brooklyn, with Fort Greene Park
nearby, and begins now, with the writing of this proposal.

I am not proposing to finish the archive, illustrate a statement I already
understand, or select the final photographs for my portfolio. I am asking for
time and space to live with a field of images long enough for its own structures,
rhythms, refusals, and invitations to become perceptible.

The proposal is an instrument of attention, not a contract. It establishes an
intention so that the part of me asking for this encounter can be heard, received,
and given a supportive place to grow. I will not be judged by whether the work
resembles what I could imagine before entering it.

## The container

- **Duration:** up to two weeks; the exact working dates remain open.
- **Site:** 196 in Brooklyn, the surrounding neighborhood, and the public-safe
  photography notebook.
- **Material:** Field Set 001, still provisional and private, with a working
  scale of approximately 1,000 photographs.
- **Conditions:** sustained attention, room to arrange and rearrange, ordinary
  life in the apartment, walks, rest, conversation, and a durable record of
  questions and changes of mind.
- **Publication state:** held. The residency may generate candidates for later
  review, but it does not clear or publish an image.

The container should be sound enough to support concentration and loose enough
to let the work move. The archive may be encountered on screens, as temporary
prints, in sequences, across a table or wall, through writing, in conversation,
or in another form that becomes necessary. None of those forms is promised.

## Proposed practice

1. **Arrive without ranking.** Meet the field broadly before deciding what is
   important, representative, beautiful, or useful.
2. **Let images become neighbors.** Arrange, separate, repeat, and reorder so
   that relationships can appear across projects, years, places, and ordinary
   intervals.
3. **Follow charge and resistance.** Notice attraction, discomfort, tenderness,
   confusion, repetition, absence, and the images that refuse the preferred
   story.
4. **Make provisional constellations.** Try sequences, image-text relations,
   spatial arrangements, and counter-sequences without naming them final.
5. **Return to sources and people.** Turn uncertain identity, chronology,
   authorship, relationship, or project meaning into research and human-review
   questions rather than filling gaps through inference.
6. **Keep a public-safe notebook.** Record what becomes visible, what remains
   unknown, how the question changes, and what should be held, deferred, or
   pursued next.

## Permission to depart

The work has explicit permission to go where it needs to go. No proposal can
know beforehand what sustained encounter with the actual material will require.
Changing course is evidence that attention is operating, not a failure to
execute the plan.

The first pass might become a sequence, a wall, a book dummy, an image-and-text
essay, a performance, an installation question, a photo-editor brief, a revised
method, a set of research leads, or no public-facing work at all. It may remain
private. It may reveal that the first question was too small, too certain, or
simply not the question the archive needed.

The Teju Cole residency story matters here as a precedent for this permission.
Cole went to Zurich intending to work on a nonfiction manuscript about Lagos.
He did some writing, but attention to Switzerland redirected much of the
residency toward wandering and photography; that work later developed into
*Fernweh*. The useful lesson is not that intention is disposable. It is that a
residency can honor its intention by making enough room for attention to change
the work.

## What would count as a successful first pass

Success does not require a finished artifact, a fixed number of selects, or a
public result. A successful residency would mean:

- the promised time and attention were genuinely given;
- the photographs were allowed to complicate the starting language;
- provisional constellations and counter-readings were recorded without being
  mistaken for conclusions;
- new source, project, relationship, and visual-research questions became
  legible;
- the next encounter with the archive became more possible;
- privacy, provenance, collective credit, rights, consent, and represented
  people's dignity remained intact;
- an honest outcome of no publication candidates remained acceptable.

## Freedom and care

The proposal protects artistic freedom inside the practice, not freedom from
responsibility to the people and histories present in the photographs. Play
does not override privacy, provenance, attribution, rights, consent, factual
review, or Jamie's approval for an exact public use.

That distinction is part of the residency's hospitality. The work is free to
change form because the container keeps its ethical boundaries. A private image
can transform the artist's understanding without ever becoming public.

## Working acceptance

> I receive and accept this proposal. The residency starts with its writing.
> The artist has permission to wander, change course, rest, return, and follow
> the work where it needs to go. No finished work is owed. The promise is to
> give the voice asking for this time a real place in which to be heard.

This working acceptance records the spirit of 196. It is not a legal agreement,
an asset approval, or a substitute for Jamie's final authorship review.

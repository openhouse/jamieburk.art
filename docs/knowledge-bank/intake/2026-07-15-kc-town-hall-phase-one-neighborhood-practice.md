## Phase One Field Delivery

Jamie confirms that he served in the day-to-day general-contractor and project-
management function for Phase One. He describes arriving first each morning
with measured drawings and survey cards, coordinating work from the basement to
the scaffolding and roof deck, and managing dependencies among historic masonry,
roofing, carpentry, welding, engineering, architecture, plumbing, and other
specialist work. One example was sequencing the TPO roof membrane with restored
parapet masonry and historic ceramic caps. He confirms that Phase One was
completed in 2019 and recalls getting the roof on before the first snow.

The packet independently names Jamie as **Founder, Project Manager, Artist &
Community Organizer, New Media Consultant**. It identifies a multidisciplinary
team including historic-masonry, architecture, roofing, concrete, electrical,
and legal specialists. It defines Phase One as a cold-shell effort including
roof structure and membrane, structural masonry, floor framing, water, debris
removal, access, safety, and related work. It reports 2018 roof replacement and
major structural-masonry progress and states a planned 2019 Phase One
completion.

The distinction is important: the packet was assembled before the end of 2019.
It corroborates Jamie's title, team, scope, 2018 progress, and completion plan;
Jamie's firsthand account is the current basis for saying Phase One was actually
completed in 2019. “General contractor” describes the function Jamie says he
performed. It is not evidence of licensure or a formal contractual title, and it
does not absorb the specialist work or credit of the project team.

## Neighborhood Survey System

Jamie confirms that he designed a 4-by-6 handbill asking residents what they
wanted at 36th and Indiana and created the backing data-collection system. He
carried survey cards during daily site work so the restoration site could also
function as a continuing listening surface.

The proposal packet reproduces the instrument and says the team was conducting
the survey with Oak Park Neighborhood Association and New Horizon Missionary
Baptist Church. It states that ongoing results directly shaped the proposal. A
January 2019 letter from Council Member Scott Taylor independently describes
the neighborhood process as impressive and says the survey directly influenced
the proposed retail uses. A separate Julia Cole letter describes Jamie and
Julia Fredenburg as actively engaging neighbors and organizations through a
listening-driven process intended to guide the renovation.

This establishes a concrete participation system and its role in proposal
formation. It does not establish the number of responses, a representative
sample, community consensus, or implementation of every requested use.

## Tired Of Tires

Jamie confirms that, as an Oak Park Neighborhood Association member, he
designed and coordinated the initial monthly free Tired of Tires program with
city services. He says he personally routed pickups, collected and delivered
tires to the city's recycling center, recorded monthly counts, and distributed
printed information for Tired of Tires, KC Town Hall's survey, Oak Park's
dumpster day, Cleveland Ave Unify to Beautify, and the Chestnut Street Resource
Center's dumpster day. He says service later expanded to Indian Mound.

The existing public-account census independently establishes a May 2019 launch
with Oak Park and a recurring East Kansas City program record. It contains 99
surviving account-authored Tired of Tires records through 2022. That public
record corroborates the program and its continuity, not Jamie's authorship or
operation of every post or later pickup. Jamie's exact operating period, the
Indian Mound expansion, monthly pickup totals, tire totals, and handoff remain
open for reconciliation with spreadsheets, city records, print artifacts, and
collaborator accounts.

## Cleveland Avenue

Jamie identifies himself as a co-founding contributor to HENC's Cleveland Ave
Unify to Beautify program. He credits Pastor Lee with the originating insight:
Cleveland Avenue connected multiple East Side neighborhood-association areas
and could give residents and city leaders a shared way to perceive, discuss,
and invest in the corridor.

Jamie says he contributed the identity and logo, photography, social account,
listening-session maps, resident-reporting surfaces, and pro bono printing of
hundreds of handbills. This is retained as a bounded firsthand research lead.
The knowledge bank does not yet claim that the program caused a particular
capital allocation. HENC, Pastor Lee, neighborhood associations, residents,
elected officials, and other contributors retain collective credit.

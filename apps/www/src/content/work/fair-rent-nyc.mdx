## Context

NYC Artist Coalition / FairRentNYC / Commercial Rent Stabilization work sits inside a civic and coalition environment where public policy, cultural-space advocacy, storefront stability, arts communities, city and state strategy, legal questions, lived experience, and public data all meet.

In that kind of work, documentation is not clerical aftercare. It is infrastructure: a way to preserve shared memory, source trails, decisions, and next steps without leaking protected context.

## What was unclear

The work mixed public materials, private notes, legal and policy questions, public-data framing, city/state strategy lanes, meeting follow-ups, and sensitive coalition context. The operating problem was to make the work easier to remember and continue while protecting strategy, legal review, unapproved quotes, and stakeholder lists.

## What became usable

Jamie built and stewarded 30+ pages of shared campaign-memory and coordination infrastructure: running minutes, action trackers, decision records, source maps, public-data framing, stakeholder follow-up notes, and public-safe explanations that helped collaborators continue the work.

The shared layer separated known public material from open questions and protected context. Collaborators could see what had happened, what needed review, what could be explained publicly, and what should remain private.

Jamie also co-founded NYC Artist Coalition and built public campaign web surfaces for cultural-space advocacy, including the coalition site, FairRentNYC, Talks Not Raids, and Let NYC Dance. That is a direct co-founder and web-infrastructure claim. It does not turn collective campaign accomplishments into a solo claim.

## What I did

Jamie built the documentation architecture, synthesized meetings into decision records and action trackers, created a legislative source map and provenance redline, translated complex policy and public-data questions into usable materials, and maintained source-backed context for collaborators.

On the public web side, Jamie's role was more direct: co-founding NYC Artist Coalition, building campaign websites, and creating public-facing information surfaces. Across Cabaret Law repeal, Office of Nightlife, nightlife-enforcement reporting, Commercial Rent Stabilization, and storefront-stability work, he built and stewarded operating and communications infrastructure; movement and policy outcomes remain collective.

## How people used it

Collaborators could use the shared memory to orient after meetings, find source context, track next steps, identify unresolved legal or policy questions, and prepare more public-safe explanations of complex civic issues.

The system also helped manage the boundary between public communication and private coordination. That boundary is part of the work. A good civic documentation system should make it easier to share what can be shared and easier to protect what should not be published.

## Outcome / evidence

The proof is a body of campaign-memory infrastructure: running minutes, decision records, action trackers, source maps, legal/policy question logs, public-data framing, and stakeholder next steps.

Because this is active and sensitive civic work, the evidence is described as a public-safe summary. Private notes, legal-review materials, raw strategy documents, private stakeholder lists, private emails, and unapproved quotes are intentionally omitted.

## Transferable proof

This case matters for technical project management because it shows how to create operating structure when goals are important, stakeholders are many, and the work cannot rely on memory alone.

It matters for product operations because it treats documentation as an operating system: a place where feedback, decisions, sources, risks, and adoption context can be gathered and reused.

It matters for knowledge systems and AI-readiness because it models a source-backed memory practice with human review, boundary-setting, and explicit Known / Open / Protected layers.

## Context

FairRentNYC / Commercial Rent Stabilization work sits inside a civic and coalition environment where public policy, storefront stability, arts communities, city and state strategy, legal questions, lived experience, public web infrastructure, and public data all meet.

In that kind of work, a website is not just a poster and documentation is not clerical aftercare. Both are infrastructure. Without public campaign surfaces, people do not know where to go. Without a shared memory system, meetings fragment, source trails become hard to trust, action items disappear, private context leaks into places it should not, and collaborators lose the thread of what has already been clarified.

## What was unclear

The work involved many stakeholders and different kinds of knowledge: public campaign sites, public materials, private notes, legal and policy questions, legislative provenance, public-data framing, city/state strategy lanes, meeting follow-ups, story consent, and sensitive coalition context.

The risk was not only disorganization. The risk was overexposure. A civic documentation system has to help collaborators remember and act while still protecting private strategy, legal-review context, unapproved quotes, private stakeholder lists, and materials that should not become public.

## What became usable

Jamie built or materially shaped public-facing campaign web infrastructure for NYC Artist Coalition and related advocacy efforts, including public sites for [NYC Artist Coalition](https://nycartc.com/), [FairRentNYC](https://fairrentnyc.nycartc.com/), [Let NYC Dance](https://letnycdance.nycartc.com/), and [Talks Not Raids](https://talksnotraids.com/).

He also helped build and steward a shared operating-memory system: running minutes, action trackers, decision records, source and provenance maps, open-question registers, city/state strategy lanes, public-data framing, stakeholder follow-up notes, and public-safe explanations that could help collaborators continue the work.

The strongest proof has two faces: public campaign infrastructure and shared campaign memory. The public sites made coalition priorities findable, navigable, and actionable. The shared working memory helped separate known public material from open questions and protected context. Together, they made it easier to see what could be explained publicly, what needed review, and what should remain private.

## What I did

Jamie contributed to the campaign web architecture, public information architecture, documentation architecture, meeting memory, action tracking, and source-backed context for collaborators. He translated complex policy, public-data, and coalition questions into public pages, internal reference systems, and usable materials.

The work required careful language discipline. This page uses collective-work language on purpose: built campaign web infrastructure, contributed to, helped structure, built and stewarded shared documentation, supported coordination, and translated complex questions into usable materials. It does not claim that Jamie led the movement, owned the bill, provided official legal analysis, or single-handedly created the campaign.

The public-data work is a good example of the role. Jamie helped frame privacy-preserving ways to ask for aggregate vacancy, occupancy, and lease-cost indicators without exposing individual tenants, owners, leases, legal strategy, or private coalition records.

## How people used it

Public audiences could use campaign sites to understand the issue, find public documents, follow calls to action, and see how cultural-space concerns connected to nightlife enforcement, dancing, storefront vacancy, and commercial rent. Collaborators could use the shared memory to orient after meetings, find source context, track next steps, identify unresolved legal or policy questions, separate city and state strategy lanes, and prepare more public-safe explanations of complex civic issues.

The system also helped manage the boundary between public communication and private coordination. That boundary is part of the work. A good civic documentation system should make it easier to share what can be shared and easier to protect what should not be published.

## Outcome / evidence

The proof is a body of campaign web and campaign-memory infrastructure: public advocacy sites, action surfaces, public explainers, running minutes, decision records, action trackers, source and provenance maps, open-question registers, legal/policy question logs, consent-aware story-handling notes, privacy-preserving public-data framing, and stakeholder next steps.

Because this is active and sensitive civic work, the evidence is described as a public-safe summary. Private notes, legal-review materials, raw strategy documents, backend/admin materials, private analytics, contact lists, private stakeholder lists, private emails, and unapproved quotes are intentionally omitted.

## Transferable proof

This case matters for technical project management because it shows how to create operating structure when goals are important, stakeholders are many, and the work cannot rely on memory alone.

It matters for product operations because it treats public websites and documentation as operating systems: places where action, feedback, decisions, sources, risks, and adoption context can be gathered and reused.

It matters for knowledge systems and AI-readiness because it models a source-backed memory practice with human review, boundary-setting, and explicit Known / Open / Protected layers.

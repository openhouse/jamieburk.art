## Context

NYC Artist Coalition / FairRentNYC / Commercial Rent Stabilization work sits inside a civic and coalition environment where public policy, cultural-space advocacy, storefront stability, arts communities, city and state strategy, legal questions, lived experience, and public data all meet.

In that kind of work, documentation is not clerical aftercare. It is infrastructure. Without a shared memory system, meetings fragment, source trails become hard to trust, action items disappear, private context leaks into places it should not, and collaborators lose the thread of what has already been clarified.

## What was unclear

The work involved many stakeholders and different kinds of knowledge: public materials, private notes, legal and policy questions, public-data framing, city/state strategy lanes, meeting follow-ups, and sensitive coalition context.

The risk was not only disorganization. The risk was overexposure. A civic documentation system has to help collaborators remember and act while still protecting private strategy, legal-review context, unapproved quotes, private stakeholder lists, and materials that should not become public.

## What became usable

I helped build and steward a shared campaign-memory system: running minutes, action trackers, decision records, source maps, public-data framing, stakeholder follow-up notes, and public-safe explanations that could help collaborators continue the work.

The strongest proof is the 30+ pages of shared campaign-memory infrastructure. Those pages helped separate known public material from open questions and protected context. They made it easier to see what had happened, what needed review, what could be explained publicly, and what should remain private.

I also co-founded NYC Artist Coalition and built public campaign web surfaces for cultural-space advocacy, including the coalition site, FairRentNYC, Talks Not Raids, and Let NYC Dance. The campaign outcomes belong to the coalition and its collaborators.

## What I did

I contributed to the documentation architecture, structured meeting memory, translated complex policy and public-data questions into usable materials, supported action tracking, and created source-backed context for collaborators. On the public web side, my role was more direct: co-founding NYC Artist Coalition, building campaign websites, and creating public-facing information surfaces.

The broader coalition work includes campaigns around Cabaret Law repeal, Office of Nightlife creation, nightlife enforcement reporting, Commercial Rent Stabilization, and storefront stability. My documented contribution is the operating and communications infrastructure around those efforts; it is not a claim of sole leadership, legal authorship, or individual ownership of collective outcomes.

## How people used it

Collaborators could use the shared memory to orient after meetings, find source context, track next steps, identify unresolved legal or policy questions, and prepare more public-safe explanations of complex civic issues.

The system also helped manage the boundary between public communication and private coordination. That boundary is part of the work. A good civic documentation system should make it easier to share what can be shared and easier to protect what should not be published.

## Outcome / evidence

The proof is a body of campaign-memory infrastructure: running minutes, decision records, action trackers, source maps, legal/policy question logs, public-data framing, stakeholder next steps, and maintained campaign web surfaces.

## Transferable proof

The transferable pattern treats documentation as an operating system for stakeholder-heavy work: a place where feedback, decisions, sources, risks, and next actions can be reviewed and reused, with human judgment and explicit boundaries around what remains protected.

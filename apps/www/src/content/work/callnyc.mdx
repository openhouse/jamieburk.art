## Institutional context

On January 30, 2016, the New York City Council convened a constituent-services hackathon at Civic Hall.<Cite evidence={["EVID-CALL-CIVICHALL-DATE", "EVID-CALL-NYCCOUNCIL-VENUE"]} /> Civic Hall announced the session for 1-3 p.m.,<Cite evidence="EVID-CALL-CIVICHALL-HOURS" /> and the Council described it that day as its first CouncilStat hackathon.<Cite evidence="EVID-CALL-NYCCOUNCIL-FIRST-COUNCILSTAT" />

A New York City Council-branded promotional graphic used the wording "New York City Council Hackathon." That wording is treated here as recovered event branding, not as proof of a longer formal registration title.<Cite evidence="EVID-CALL-PROMOTIONAL-BRANDING" />

Participants previewed constituent-services data recorded through CouncilStat and considered how district-office operations and public data could become more usable and transparent.<Cite evidence="EVID-CALL-COUNCIL-DATA-PURPOSE" />

After the complete dataset became public, Jamie independently built CallNYC.org as a resident-facing interpretation of the records.<Cite evidence="EVID-CALL-POLITICO-INDEPENDENT-FOLLOW-ON" />

## What was unclear

Constituent-services data carried civic signal, but residents needed a translation layer. What issue is this? Which office or district context might matter? What next step is reasonable? What should be treated as general guidance rather than official service direction?

## What became usable

The prototype organized released data into issue pathways, district context, and resident-facing guidance. It treated data translation as a product and information-architecture problem: the resident should not have to understand the whole dataset before seeing a possible next step.

## What I did

Jamie independently built the prototype, shaped the issue pathways, translated data into public-facing guidance patterns, and connected civic-data structure to a resident-readable interface.

The work required choosing what not to claim. A prototype can demonstrate a pattern without pretending to be an official service.

## Data limits

CouncilStat records were administrative records, not a uniform performance-measurement system. Council staff cautioned that offices used the application differently and entered information with different frequency. A larger recorded caseload could not safely be read as a simple measure of office effectiveness, specialization, or resident need.<Cite evidence="EVID-CALL-POLITICO-LIMITATIONS" />

## Outcome / evidence

Politico New York covered CallNYC in March 2016 as a website for exploring City Council constituent-services data.<Cite evidence="EVID-CALL-POLITICO-COVERAGE" /> The public repository preserves the implementation history.<Cite evidence="EVID-CALL-GITHUB-REPOSITORY" />

## Transferable proof

This case matters for civic technology and public-facing tools because it shows open-data translation into a usable public interface.

It matters for product operations because the work moved from dataset to user pathway, with disclaimers and scope boundaries built into the surface.

It matters for implementation because it demonstrates a small but complete civic prototype: data source, information architecture, interface, and public-safe guidance.

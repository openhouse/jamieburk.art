## Context

On January 30, 2016, the New York City Council held a 1-3 p.m. constituent-services hackathon at Civic Hall<Cite noteId="callnyc-event-date-time" />. A Council-branded promotional graphic called it the "New York City Council Hackathon," while an event-day Council post identified it as the Council's first #CouncilStat hackathon<Cite noteId="callnyc-event-branding-councilstat" />.

Jamie participated in the workshop and later independently developed CallNYC.org after the complete CouncilStat dataset became public<Cite noteId="callnyc-participation-follow-on" />. The archived prototype reorganized constituent-service records into issue-oriented pages and office contact paths while making the limitations of inconsistent administrative records explicit<Cite noteId="callnyc-product-method" refId="callnyc-opening-method" />.

A participant photograph documents a "Digital District" discussion whose placard invited attendees to "Help improve City Council District office operations"<Cite noteId="callnyc-digital-district-photo" />. The image is not published here: rights, consent, caption, and crop review remain open, and the placard does not establish the formal title, complete agenda, facilitator, or roster of the full event.

## What was unclear

CouncilStat data contained civic signal, but a public dataset was not yet a resident pathway. A person still needed to understand what an issue meant, which office or district context might matter, what next step was reasonable, and where the source data could mislead.

Different Council offices used CouncilStat differently and with differing frequency. The records could reveal patterns, but could not responsibly be presented as a simple measure of office performance, specialization, or resident need. Jamie's product judgment included deciding what the data could not prove<Cite noteId="callnyc-product-method" refId="callnyc-data-limits" />.

## What I did

Jamie built the independent prototype, organized records into issue-oriented pathways, and designed pages for search, sharing, and office contact. He translated administrative categories into public-facing choices without presenting the result as official City guidance.

The work joined implementation with evidence discipline: source limits were part of the interface and editorial logic, not a disclaimer added after the product decisions had already been made.

## What became usable

CallNYC turned a difficult administrative dataset into issue pages, district context, and possible next steps. Residents did not need to understand the entire source table before entering through a recognizable concern.

The prototype also gave civic-technology collaborators a concrete surface for discussing how public records might become useful without promising more certainty than the records contained.

## Archival method

No Civic Hall calendar listing or dedicated event-detail page was recovered in the documented archival search<Cite noteId="callnyc-calendar-search-limit" />. That is a bounded negative finding, not a claim that no listing ever existed. The surviving Wayback page carries embedded public posts; it is not the event's recovered calendar listing.

## Transferable proof

For product and operations teams, the transferable pattern is direct: map a difficult source to the decisions a person needs to make, build a bounded pathway, and keep the source limitations inside the operating system people actually use.

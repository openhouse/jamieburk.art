## What was unclear

The data carried civic signal, but a public dataset was not yet a resident pathway. A person still needed to understand what an issue meant, which office or district context might matter, what next step was reasonable, and where the source data could mislead.

## What I did

I built the independent prototype, organized records into issue-oriented pathways, and translated administrative categories into public-facing guidance patterns. The interface let a person enter through a recognizable concern instead of first having to understand the complete dataset.

The work also required choosing what not to claim. CallNYC was not commissioned by the Council, documented as a formal hackathon submission, or presented as an official City service.

## What became usable

The prototype organized constituent-services records into issue pages, district context, and possible next steps. It treated data translation as a product and information-architecture problem: a resident should not have to interpret the whole dataset before finding an entry point.

For product and operations teams, the transferable pattern is direct: map a difficult source to the decisions a person needs to make, build a bounded pathway, and keep source limitations inside the product rather than in a separate disclaimer.

## Outcome and evidence

Politico New York covered CallNYC on March 14, 2016, connecting my independent project to the January gathering and the later fuller CouncilStat data release.<Cite pageId="callnyc" occurrenceId="press-coverage" />

The public repository preserves the surviving implementation. The current portfolio presents CallNYC as historical evidence of civic-data translation, public interface delivery, and careful scope-setting.

## Transferable proof

The transferable pattern moves from open dataset to bounded user pathway: source data, information architecture, interface, iteration, public documentation, and scope limits working together in one small public prototype.

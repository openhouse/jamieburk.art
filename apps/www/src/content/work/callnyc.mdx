A Council-branded promotional graphic used the title "New York City Council Hackathon."<Cite setId="callnyc-case-study" claimId="callnyc-event-branding" occurrence={1} /> Jamie's participant archive separately documents a breakout prompt titled "Digital District - Help improve City Council District office operations."<Cite setId="callnyc-case-study" claimId="callnyc-digital-district-breakout" occurrence={1} /> No complete agenda, breakout roster, or dedicated Civic Hall event page has been recovered.<Cite setId="callnyc-case-study" claimId="callnyc-no-dedicated-listing-recovered" occurrence={1} />

The public problem was familiar: data existed, but the path from a resident's issue to a useful next step was not always easy to see. A dataset can be technically public and still be hard for a person to use.

## What was unclear

Constituent-services records carried civic signal, but residents needed a translation layer. What issue is this? Which office or district context might matter? What next step is reasonable? What should be treated as general guidance rather than official service direction?

The data also needed careful interpretation. A count is not self-explanatory, and an interface can make an unsupported comparison look more authoritative than it is.

## What became usable

After the full CouncilStat dataset became public, Jamie independently developed CallNYC.org as a resident-facing interpretation of the records.<Cite setId="callnyc-case-study" claimId="callnyc-independent-follow-on" occurrence={1} /> The result was an archived, unofficial prototype rather than a Council product or current City service.<Cite setId="callnyc-case-study" claimId="callnyc-archived-unofficial-status" occurrence={1} />

The prototype organized open data into issue pathways, district context, and possible next steps. It treated data translation as a product and information-architecture problem: a resident should not have to understand the whole dataset before finding an entry point.

Council offices used CouncilStat differently and with different frequency. Recorded case counts therefore could not responsibly be treated as a simple measure of office performance, specialization, or resident need.<Cite setId="callnyc-case-study" claimId="callnyc-data-comparability-limits" occurrence={1} />

## What I did

I built the prototype, shaped the issue pathways, translated data into public-facing guidance patterns, and connected civic-data structure to a resident-readable interface.

The work also required choosing what not to claim. A prototype can demonstrate a pattern without pretending to be an official service, and public data can invite exploration without supporting every comparison a reader might make.

## What the prototype offered

The prototype offered residents and civic-technology observers a way to explore how an issue might connect to district context, constituent-service categories, or possible civic next steps.

For product and operations teams, the transferable pattern is the same one visible elsewhere in my work: take a scattered or expert-facing source, map what a user needs to do, and create a pathway that is easier to enter.

## Outcome / evidence

Politico New York covered CallNYC in March 2016 as a website for exploring newly released City Council constituent-services data. The public repository preserves the implementation, while the source notes below keep the event history, protected participant evidence, and negative archival findings within their proper boundaries.

CallNYC is historical. It is not an official or current City Council service, legal advice, emergency guidance, or comprehensive current civic guidance.<Cite setId="callnyc-case-study" claimId="callnyc-archived-unofficial-status" occurrence={2} />

## Transferable proof

This case matters for civic technology because it shows open-data translation into a usable public interface without hiding the limits of the underlying records.

It matters for product operations because the work moved from dataset to user pathway, with disclaimers and scope boundaries built into the surface.

It matters for implementation because it demonstrates a small but complete civic prototype: data source, information architecture, interface, public guidance, and an explicit boundary around what the tool could not establish.

## Context

On January 30, 2016, Civic Hall hosted a 1-3 p.m. New York City Council hackathon focused on constituent services. The Council described the gathering on the event day as its first CouncilStat hackathon.<Cite pageKey="callnyc" id="callnyc-event-date-time" />

Participant photography documents a "Digital District" breakout focused on improving City Council district-office operations. No full agenda, breakout roster, registration contents, or dedicated Civic Hall event page has been recovered.<Cite pageKey="callnyc" id="callnyc-digital-district" />

After the complete CouncilStat dataset was released, I independently built CallNYC.org as a resident-facing interpretation of the records.<Cite pageKey="callnyc" id="callnyc-independent-follow-on" />

## What was unclear

Constituent-services data carried civic signal, but residents needed a translation layer. What issue is this? Which district context might matter? What next step is reasonable? What should be treated as general guidance rather than official service direction?

The project also needed a strong disclaimer. CallNYC was an independent project, not an official City Council service, not a current civic service, not legal guidance, and not a replacement for emergency or agency channels. It was a civic-tech prototype exploring how public data could become more navigable.

## What became usable

The prototype organized open data into issue pathways, district context, and resident-facing guidance. It treated data translation as a product and information-architecture problem: the resident should not have to understand the whole dataset before seeing a next step.

Because district offices used CouncilStat differently, the project treated case counts as evidence of recorded activity, not as a simple measure of office performance.<Cite pageKey="callnyc" id="callnyc-data-limitations" />

The public-safe framing today is historical. The project shows civic-data translation, open-data product thinking, public interface design, and the ability to move from a hackathon context into a working prototype.

## What I did

Jamie built the prototype, shaped the issue pathways, translated data into public-facing guidance patterns, and connected civic-data structure to a resident-readable interface.

The work required choosing what not to claim. A prototype can demonstrate a pattern without pretending to be an official service. The page therefore keeps the archived status visible and avoids current-service language.

## How people used it

Residents and civic-tech observers could see how an issue might connect to district context, constituent-service categories, or possible civic next steps. The project also gave collaborators a concrete surface for discussing what useful public guidance could look like.

For product and operations teams, the transferable pattern is the same one visible elsewhere in Jamie's work: take a scattered or expert-facing source, map what a user needs to do, and create a pathway that is easier to enter.

## Outcome / evidence

The project was a historical civic-tech demo built from CouncilStat / constituent-services open data. It included public issue pages, resident-facing guidance, and civic-data interpretation after the January 2016 CouncilStat hackathon context.

Politico New York covered CallNYC in 2016 as a website for exploring City Council constituent-services data.

## Transferable proof

This case matters for civic technology and public-facing tools because it shows open-data translation into a usable public interface.

It matters for product operations because the work moved from dataset to user pathway, with disclaimers and scope boundaries built into the surface.

It matters for implementation because it demonstrates a small but complete civic prototype: data source, information architecture, interface, and public-safe guidance.

## Context

On January 30, 2016, Civic Hall advertised a 1-3 p.m. New York City Council hackathon focused on constituent services.<Cite page={callNycCitationPage} claim="claim.callnyc.hackathon.date-time-purpose" /> The New York City Council posted from Civic Hall that day and described the gathering as the first CouncilStat hackathon.<Cite page={callNycCitationPage} claim="claim.callnyc.hackathon.first-councilstat" /> Public Civic Hall materials framed the event around an NYC Council hackathon connected to constituent services and CouncilStat.<Cite page={callNycCitationPage} claim="claim.callnyc.hackathon.event-branding" />

A participant photograph also documents a breakout called Digital District, with the prompt: "Help improve City Council District office operations."<Cite page={callNycCitationPage} claim="claim.callnyc.hackathon.digital-district-breakout" /> That is the bounded public claim: Digital District is supported as a breakout label and prompt, not as the full event title.

## What Was Unclear

Constituent-service data carried civic signal, but the resident-facing path was not obvious. A dataset can be public and still be hard to use. What issue is this? Which district context matters? What next step is reasonable? Which statements belong on a public prototype, and which would wrongly imply official service direction?

Council offices also used CouncilStat differently, so case counts are imperfect operational traces, not simple performance measures.<Cite page={callNycCitationPage} claim="claim.callnyc.project.data-limitations" /> The work needed to make the data more navigable without pretending the numbers could rank offices, diagnose performance, or replace current service channels.

## What Became Usable

After the full dataset release, Jamie independently developed CallNYC.org as an archived civic prototype organizing constituent-service records into issue-oriented pages.<Cite page={callNycCitationPage} claim="claim.callnyc.project.independent-follow-on" />

The prototype treated civic-data translation as a product and information-architecture problem. It organized issue pathways, district context, and resident-readable next steps while keeping the unofficial status visible. The point was not to become the City Council. The point was to show how a public dataset could become easier for a resident to enter.

## What I Did

Jamie built the prototype, shaped the issue pathways, translated constituent-service data into public-facing guidance patterns, and connected civic-data structure to a resident-readable interface.

The work required choosing what not to claim. CallNYC was not an official City Council service, not a current civic service, not legal guidance, not emergency guidance, and not a replacement for agency or elected-office channels.

## Outcome / Evidence

CallNYC is historical civic-tech work from 2016: an archived, unofficial prototype built from constituent-service open data, public coverage, and a public code record. It matters because it shows a recurring pattern in Jamie's work: find the emerging structure inside a messy public system, give it a usable form, and keep the limits visible.

## Transferable Proof

This case matters for civic technology because it shows open-data translation into a public interface.

It matters for product operations because the work moved from dataset to user pathway, with disclaimers and scope boundaries built into the surface.

It matters for implementation because it demonstrates a small but complete civic prototype: source context, information architecture, interface, public explanation, and public-safe citation discipline.
